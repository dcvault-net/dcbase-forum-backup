Well, the answer's lie in "The Origin of Species". Since I can't get you to read it, I will quote some lines from it that may help you understand.
In this particular section of Chapter X: On the Poorness of Palaeontological Collections
Charles Darwin wrote:One remark is here worth a passing notice. During periods of elevation the area of the land and of the adjoining shoal parts of the sea will be increased, and new stations will often be formed:Ã¢â‚¬â€œ all circumstances favourable, as previously explained, for the formation of new varieties and species; but during such periods there will generally be a blank in the geological record. On the other hand, during subsidence, the inhabited area and number of inhabitants will decrease (excepting on the shores of a continent when first broken up into an archipelago), and consequently during subsidence, though there will be much extinction, few new varieties or species will be formed; and it is during these very periods of subsidence, that the deposits which are richest in fossils have been accumulated.


Pretty much, he is saying this: Large sediment deposits relates to large environmental change. You see fossils in these large deposits because the environmental change has devastated certain species that were not well adapted to the change.

What you won't see is fossils from successful intermediate varieties, because they don't die in mass numbers (remember, they are successful).

In fact, the only times that geologists really find fossils is during times of extinction. The times inbetween extinction have very few or no remains.

In the section: On the Absence of Numerous Intermediate Varieties in any Single Formation
Charles Darwin wrote:It is allÃ¢â‚¬â€œimportant to remember that naturalists have no golden rule by which to distinguish species and varieties; they grant some little variability to each species, but when they meet with a somewhat greater amount of difference between any two forms, they rank both as species, unless they are enabled to connect them together by the closest intermediate gradations...It is notorious on what excessively slight differences many palaeontologists have founded their species.

In other words, they have found intermediate varieties...it's just that they call them species when they are probably just varieties of the same species. There is really no specific method that is used to determine if two different types of animal are simply a variety of one another or a different species. Naturalists don't believe in natural selection, and therefore don't have any reason to believe that two different animals are simply varieties of one another. They just mark it down as a different species.
